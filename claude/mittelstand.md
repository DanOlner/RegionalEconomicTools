# The Mittelstand, the UK, and Tom Riordan's Northern brief

## 1. What the Mittelstand actually is

The German [Mittelstand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittelstand) is widely (and wrongly) glossed in English-language policy as "Germany's SME sector." The German federal economics ministry's own framing is more careful: it is presented as a [*model for success*](https://www.bundeswirtschaftsministerium.de/Redaktion/EN/Dossier/sme-policy.html) defined as much by ownership and ethos as by size — Ludwig Erhard's line that the Mittelstand is "much more of an ethos and a fundamental disposition" than a quantitative measure still gets quoted approvingly.

The defining bundle of features, drawn together across the [academic literature](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11301-021-00253-6) and trade summaries like [English Business](https://englishbusiness.de/en/blog/the-german-mittelstand/) and [Family Business United](https://www.familybusinessunited.com/post/how-germany-s-family-firms-power-the-mittelstand):

-   **Unity of ownership and management** — the IFM Bonn definition requires up to two natural persons (or their family) to hold ≥50% of shares *and* be in management.
-   **Long-term, often multi-generational orientation** — retained earnings over equity markets, resilience over quarterly maximisation.
-   **Deep regional rootedness** — concentrated in [North Rhine-Westphalia, Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittelstand); supported by *Sparkassen* (local savings banks) and *Landesbanken*, the dual apprenticeship system, and Fraunhofer-style applied research.
-   **Niche-market specialism with global exports** — often [hidden champions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_champions): world market leaders in narrow B2B niches, invisible to consumers.
-   **Lean hierarchies, low staff turnover, social responsibility to the town.**

Scale: \~99% of German firms self-identify as Mittelstand, employing \>60% of the workforce and generating more than half of national output.

## 2. What it isn't

The English-language debate consistently flattens the concept, so it's worth being explicit about what is **not** a Mittelstand firm:

-   **Not "any SME."** The standard EU SME ceiling (≤250 staff, ≤€50m turnover) is irrelevant — Bosch and Trumpf describe themselves as Mittelstand at multi-billion-euro scale. The "upper Mittelstand" runs to \~€1bn revenue.
-   **Not a size category at all, primarily.** Size is a weak proxy for the underlying governance/ethos cluster.
-   **Not VC-backed, equity-financed, or shareholder-value oriented.** The model explicitly counters [Silicon Valley-style entrepreneurship](https://www.enterpriseresearch.ac.uk/the-mittelstand-v-silicon-valley-which-model-of-entrepreneurship-is-most-relevant-for-post-brexit-britain/) — it is the antithesis of "blitzscale and exit."
-   **Not transplantable as a policy lever in isolation.** The serious critique (see [Enterprise Research Centre](https://www.enterpriseresearch.ac.uk/the-mittelstand-v-silicon-valley-which-model-of-entrepreneurship-is-most-relevant-for-post-brexit-britain/), [Scottish Policy Now](https://www.scottishpolicynow.co.uk/article/success-secrets-shared-learning-from-the-best-mittelstand-and-british-global-niche-champions/)) is that the firms are downstream of an institutional stack — vocational training, regional banks, co-determination, *Land*-level industrial policy, low real interest rates over decades — which the UK does not have and cannot summon by exhortation.
-   **Not uniformly healthy in 2026.** Even in Germany the model is under pressure from energy costs, China competition, succession gaps, and digitisation lag — a useful caveat against treating it as a static ideal.

## 3. The Mittelstand in UK analysis

The term has been imported into UK debate in three overlapping waves:

**(a) The "missing middle" / mid-market diagnosis.** A long-running CBI and BVCA argument that the UK has plenty of micro-firms and a handful of giants but a thin layer of mid-sized companies relative to peers — see [The Manufacturer](https://www.themanufacturer.com/articles/uks-mittelstand-central-to-new-economy/) and [Campden FB](http://www.campdenfb.com/article/uk-business-organisation-seeks-build-british-mittelstand). The framing is statistical: mid-sized firms (£10m–£100m turnover / 50–500 staff) are \<1% of UK businesses but generate roughly a quarter of national GVA and, per [BDO via the Northern Growth Strategy](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/northern-growth-strategy-case-for-change/northern-growth-strategy-next-steps), are projected to deliver 41% of GVA growth over the next three years.

**(b) The post-Brexit / post-Covid reshoring argument.** Chains of papers from [Enterprise Research Centre](https://www.enterpriseresearch.ac.uk/the-mittelstand-v-silicon-valley-which-model-of-entrepreneurship-is-most-relevant-for-post-brexit-britain/), [Automate UK](https://www.automate-uk.com/news-publications/insights-from-the-german-mittelstand-and-strategies-for-uk-sme-support/) and similar arguing that the UK should shift R&D tax credit, export support, patient capital and skills policy toward Mittelstand-shaped firms rather than chasing unicorns. Recurring asks: long-term debt finance, R&D incentives for incremental process innovation, technical education reform.

**(c) The "Brittelstand" critique.** Comparative work cited in the [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittelstand) and elsewhere finds that British mid-cap firms exist but behave differently — shorter management horizons, more outsourcing of production to low-cost markets, weaker apprenticeship pipelines, less regionally embedded finance. The implication: copying the label without the institutional substrate gets you a thinner thing — sometimes mockingly called a "McMittelstand."

**(d) Industrial strategy reframings.** [Grant Thornton](https://www.grantthornton.co.uk/news-centre/uks-medium-sized-firms-most-productive-but-hindered-by-inability-to-invest-and-staff-burnout/), [NatWest's Mid-Market Champions programme](https://www.natwestgroup.com/news-and-insights/news-room/press-releases/enterprise/2026/mar/natwest-appoints-mid-market-champions-to-help-drive-vital-busine.html) and PwC's regional productivity tracking all hammer the same point: mid-market firms are the UK's most productive segment, but capital-constrained, with the constraint biting hardest outside London/SE.

## 4. Tom Riordan as Northern Growth Envoy: where the overlap lives

[Riordan](https://www.centreforcities.org/about/person/tom-riordan/) was appointed by Rachel Reeves in October 2025 as the first HM Treasury envoy for Northern growth, three days a week for an initial 15 months, reporting jointly to Treasury, DfT, and MHCLG, based at the Darlington campus — see [Place Yorkshire](https://www.placeyorkshire.co.uk/treasury-appoints-first-envoy-to-drive-northern-growth/), [LGC](https://www.lgcplus.com/politics/governance-and-structure/riordan-named-envoy-for-northern-growth-29-10-2025/), [Civil Service World](https://www.civilserviceworld.com/professions/article/dhsc-second-perm-sec-to-become-envoy-for-the-north). He brings a 14-year run as Leeds City Council CEO and a stint as second permanent secretary at DHSC.

His remit covers the [**Northern Growth Strategy**](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/northern-growth-strategy), Northern Powerhouse Rail, and the proposed **Northern Growth Corridor** (an inter-city corridor modelled rhetorically on the Oxford–Cambridge Arc).

Where this overlaps with Mittelstand-shaped thinking — explicitly and implicitly:

**Explicit:** The [March 2026 *Next Steps* document](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69bbbad2e2678e3266bbe5ad/Northern_Growth_Strategy_Next_Steps.pdf) names mid-sized firms as "central to the North's economic strength, underpinning supply chains across priority IS-8 sectors" (defence, life sciences, clean energy, advanced manufacturing) and commits the government to "establish a community of high growth businesses from across the North." This is straight mid-market industrial strategy framed around supply-chain resilience — the closest the UK has come to a stated Mittelstand-style ambition at national policy level. Riordan has reportedly [convened business boards across the North in Leeds](https://www.centreforcities.org/about/person/tom-riordan/) specifically on the role of mid-sized firms.

**Implicit overlaps with the German playbook:**

1.  **Place-based, sector-anchored.** Treating South Yorkshire's [Defence Growth Deal](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/northern-growth-strategy-case-for-change/northern-growth-strategy-next-steps) (steel, metals, composites) and Liverpool's life sciences/quantum cluster as the spine — analogous to Baden-Württemberg's machine-tool clusters or NRW's chemicals belt. The unit of analysis is the city-region's specialism, not the individual firm.

2.  **Devolution as the institutional substrate.** Integrated Settlements (≥£9bn for Greater Manchester, Liverpool, West Yorkshire, North East, South Yorkshire from 2026–27 to 2029–30), the statutory "Right to Request," and a fiscal devolution roadmap together gesture toward something that could *eventually* play the role *Länder* play for the German model — durable regional capacity to back firms over decades.

    > **On *Länder*:** the 16 federated states of Germany (Bavaria, Baden-Württemberg, NRW, etc.; singular *Land*). Unlike English counties or even Mayoral Strategic Authorities, they are constitutional units with their own parliaments, tax-raising powers, control over education (including the dual vocational training system), their own development banks (*Landesbanken*), and ownership stakes in the regional *Sparkassen*. When a Baden-Württemberg machine-tool firm needs patient capital, an apprentice pipeline, or applied R&D via a Fraunhofer institute, those institutions exist at *Land* level and have been backing that firm's town for decades. English devolution is currently gesturing toward a fraction of those powers with none of the constitutional permanence.

3.  **Patient regional finance.** The [British Business Bank's NPIF II](https://www.british-business-bank.co.uk/news-and-events/news/how-npif-ii-driving-growth-across-north-two-years) and NatWest's regional Mid-Market Champions sit in roughly the slot a *Sparkasse* / *Landesbank* network occupies in Germany — though at radically smaller scale and without the cooperative ownership form.

4.  **Skills and visible regional leadership.** The mid-market survey finding that 9 in 10 firms want a visible regional leader is consistent with the Mittelstand emphasis on local social embedding; Mayors plus Riordan's envoy role are the policy answer.

**Where the overlap thins:**

-   No serious move yet on the **dual vocational training system** — arguably the single most load-bearing institution in the German story.
-   **Ownership structure** is untouched: UK policy is silent on family ownership, succession planning, or the tax treatment of patient family capital — all of which are central to *why* German firms behave the way they do.
-   **Time horizon.** Riordan's brief is 15 months; the German model is measured in generations. The strategy itself acknowledges this tension by trying to set 10-year capital plans against electoral cycles.
-   **Corridor framing vs. cluster framing.** The Oxford–Cambridge analogy is fundamentally a corridor/agglomeration story; the Mittelstand story is fundamentally a *distributed* small-town and mid-town cluster story. These are not the same geography and may pull in different directions.

## 5. Could English devolution ever be *Land*-like? The UK debate

The Mittelstand argument inevitably collapses into a devolution argument, because the institutional layer that makes the German firms work (training, regional banks, applied R&D, multi-decade industrial strategy at sub-national level) sits with the *Länder*, not Berlin. So "do we want a Mittelstand?" is really "do we want *Länder*?" — and that question has been live in UK think-tank and political debate for at least 15 years.

### The gap, in numbers

The headline statistics are stark:

-   **Tax devolution.** Only about 5% of UK revenues are collected locally; for every £1 raised in tax, 96p goes straight to the Treasury, per [New Local](https://www.newlocal.org.uk/articles/fiscal-devolution/). Local taxes account for only 19% of total local government revenue versus an OECD unitary-country average of 33.6% ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/sites/default/files/2022-12/subnational-government-in-england-international-comparison.pdf)).
-   **Spending decentralisation.** Roughly 20% of UK government expenditure is decided at subnational level, against an OECD trend in the opposite direction.
-   **Tax base composition.** UK subnational revenue is \>90% property tax (council tax + business rates), one of only five OECD countries that concentrated — *Länder* by contrast share in income, corporate, and VAT receipts under Germany's *Steuerverbund* equalisation system.
-   **Scale of commitment.** The [Centre for Cities reunification analysis](https://www.centreforcities.org/blog/what-can-german-reunification-teach-the-uk-about-levelling-up/) puts West-to-East transfers at roughly €2 trillion over 1990–2014 (\~£71bn/year), explicitly *designed to outlast changes in federal government*. The original UK Levelling Up Fund totalled £4.8bn.

### The constitutional distinction

The deeper point, made most cleanly by the [Constitution Society's *Federalism and the City*](https://consoc.org.uk/britians-cities-and-lessons-from-germany/) and reinforced in [Wikipedia's devolution overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolution_in_the_United_Kingdom): **federalism is constitutional, devolution is statutory.** *Länder* powers are entrenched in the *Grundgesetz* (Basic Law) and cannot be unilaterally withdrawn by the federal government. English Mayoral Strategic Authorities exist by Act of Parliament and could in principle be abolished by another. This permanence asymmetry is what allows a Baden-Württemberg apprentice pipeline or a *Landesbank* loan book to operate on 30-year horizons.

Germany also has formal *Länder* representation in national legislation via the *Bundesrat*, and a constitutionally mandated *Finanzausgleich* (fiscal equalisation system) that redistributes between richer and poorer states. England has nothing analogous — the closest gestures are the proposed [IPPR "Senate of the Nations and Regions"](https://labourlist.org/2026/01/burnhams-britain-king-of-the-north-rewire-westminster/) (a Burnham/IPPR Lords-replacement proposal) and ad-hoc Barnett-style settlements.

### Where the UK debate has actually gone

Roughly four camps:

1.  **Fiscal-firepower-first.** [New Local](https://www.newlocal.org.uk/articles/fiscal-devolution/) and [IPPR](https://www.ippr.org/articles/britons-back-local-leaders-with-fiscal-firepower) argue the binding constraint is money, not powers — give Mayors a share of income tax, retained business rates uplift, a tourist levy, vehicle excise, etc. This is *Land*-light: more revenue autonomy without constitutional entrenchment.

2.  **Constitutional federalism.** The [Constitution Society](https://consoc.org.uk/britians-cities-and-lessons-from-germany/) and parts of the Lib Dem / Burnham wing of Labour argue you need actual entrenched powers — a written constitution or its equivalent, with an upper chamber representing the regions. [Andy Burnham's "rewire Westminster" agenda](https://labourlist.org/2026/01/burnhams-britain-king-of-the-north-rewire-westminster/) (PR voting, abolish the Lords for a regional senate, weaken the whip) is the most prominent political version.

3.  **Capacity-and-consolidation.** [Centre for Cities](https://www.centreforcities.org/publication/everything-you-need-to-know-about-english-devolution/) and the [Bennett School "Burnham blueprint"](https://www.bennettschool.cam.ac.uk/blog/the-burnham-blueprint/) emphasise that the German system rests on durable *institutional capacity* — *Landesbanken*, Fraunhofer institutes, *Handwerkskammer* (chambers of crafts), municipal consolidation. Centre for Cities specifically points out that East Germany cut its municipalities by 65% to build *fewer, stronger* authorities; English MSAs and unitarisation are creeping in that direction but slowly.

4.  **Sceptics.** The [Tandfonline study on England's "devolution periphery"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21582041.2024.2435440) and the OECD work cited in [New Local](https://www.newlocal.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Fiscal-devolution-why-we-need-it-and-how-to-make-it-work.pdf) warn that **unfunded mandates** — devolving responsibility without resource — actively *reduce* growth. Devolving badly is worse than not devolving.

### Key criticisms of the *Länder* analogy

The serious arguments against treating Germany as a template:

-   **English regions lack identity and scale.** The *Länder* range from \~700k (Bremen) to \~18m (NRW) and have centuries of distinct legal/cultural identity. English MSAs are city-region functional economic areas with weak political identity outside (arguably) Greater Manchester and the North East. There is no English equivalent of *Land* patriotism.
-   **The UK is a four-nation union, not a federation.** Adding a fifth tier of "English regional" government raises the West Lothian Question on steroids and risks destabilising the Scotland/Wales/NI settlements that already exist.
-   **Germany's federalism is *also* under strain.** Recent reform efforts (Föderalismusreform I and II) have tried to *un-pick* tangled responsibilities — the German model is not a static success story.
-   **Reunification was a one-off political moment** ([Centre for Cities](https://www.centreforcities.org/blog/what-can-german-reunification-teach-the-uk-about-levelling-up/)). Solidarity Pact-scale transfers required a national reunification narrative; "levelling up the North" does not command the same political consensus.
-   **Spasmodic vs. designed.** The Constitution Society's phrasing — that English devolution has developed "spasmodically" compared to Germany's "explicit intentionality" — captures the deeper worry: every English settlement is a bespoke deal (Greater Manchester ≠ West Yorkshire ≠ Cornwall), making it impossible to build the cross-region institutions (a common training framework, a regional banking layer) that the German model relies on.

### What it would actually take

Synthesising across these sources, a roughly *Länder*-equivalent English settlement would need, at minimum:

1.  **Constitutional entrenchment** — devolution settlements that a future Westminster cannot unilaterally revoke.
2.  **Tax-sharing not just tax-collection** — a share of income tax / VAT receipts, not just retained business rates and council tax, with formal equalisation.
3.  **Long-horizon institutions** — regional development banks (something the [British Business Bank's NPIF II](https://www.british-business-bank.co.uk/news-and-events/news/how-npif-ii-driving-growth-across-north-two-years) gestures at), regional applied-research institutes, regional control over technical/vocational education.
4.  **Stable boundaries and consolidated authorities** — fewer, larger, more permanent sub-national units, with consistent powers across them.
5.  **A representative national chamber** — *Bundesrat*-style regional voice in national legislation.
6.  **Multi-decadal funding settlements** — comparable in ambition (if not absolute scale) to the Solidarity Pact, surviving electoral cycles.

Riordan's brief touches (1) lightly via the statutory Right-to-Request, (3) via Integrated Settlements and NPIF II, (4) via continuing MSA rollout. It does not touch (2), (5), or (6) at all. Which is why the gap between the rhetoric of a "British Mittelstand" and the institutional substrate that would actually produce one remains the central tension in the strategy.

## 6. The skills pipeline: anatomy of the load-bearing institution

§4 named the **dual vocational training system** as "arguably the single most load-bearing institution in the German story," and §5 established *why* it sits where it does: education in Germany is a constitutional *Land* competence, not a federal one. This section opens that box, because the claim is more specific than "Germany has good apprenticeships." The Mittelstand's defining behaviours — incremental process innovation, low staff turnover, retention over poaching, the nerve to specialise in a narrow globally-traded niche — are *downstream of a skills pipeline* that reliably delivers certified, occupation-specific technicians to a firm in a small town, and that lets the firm treat training as an investment rather than a cost it will lose to a competitor. Take the pipeline away and the behaviours unravel. The companion notes ([universities_industrial_strategy_devolution.md](universities_industrial_strategy_devolution.md), §2; [uk_industrial_strategy_since_launch.md](uk_industrial_strategy_since_launch.md)) both circle this institution; here it is set out end-to-end so the UK comparison can be precise rather than gestural.

### The pipeline, end to end

It is genuinely a *pipeline* — a sequence of institutions that hand off to each other, with progression routes at every stage — not a menu of programmes:

1.  **The dual apprenticeship (*duale Ausbildung*).** The core. It combines on-the-job training in the firm (typically 3–4 days a week) with a part-time state vocational school, the *Berufsschule* (1–2 days a week), over two to three years; the apprentice is an employee on a training contract, paid an allowance set under collective bargaining. Around [70% of all German vocational training](https://www.bibb.de/en/77203.php) runs through this dual form, and roughly half of each youth cohort enters it ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/vocational-education-and-training-systems-in-nine-countries_1a86eb6c-en/full-report/vocational-education-and-training-in-germany_dae78944.html), [deutschland.de explainer](https://www.deutschland.de/en/topic/business/how-germanys-dual-vocational-training-system-works)) — an order of magnitude more weight than the UK places on work-based learning for the young.
2.  **Certification by the chambers.** The final examination is set and marked not by the school or central government but by the [chambers](https://www.dihk.de/en/overview-of-apprenticeships-174888) — the *IHK* (industry and commerce) and the *Handwerkskammer* (skilled crafts) — which issue a nationally portable chamber certificate. Because the qualification is the *chamber's*, not the employer's, it travels: a technician trained in one Mittelstand firm is credentialed for the whole occupation, which is precisely what makes training-without-poaching-loss rational (see financing, below).
3.  **Advancement (*Aufstiegsfortbildung*) and the *Meister*.** The pipeline does not dead-end at journeyman level. The master-craftsman qualification (*Meister*) and its commercial equivalents now sit at [EQF/DQR level 6 — formally "Bachelor Professional"](https://www.cedefop.europa.eu/en/tools/nqfs-online-tool/countries/germany-2020) — entitling the holder to run a firm, to *employ and train apprentices*, and to [enrol in a bachelor's programme at a university of applied sciences or university](https://eurydice.eacea.ec.europa.eu/national-education-systems/germany/national-qualifications-framework). The vocational track is a ladder, not a siding.
4.  **Universities of applied sciences (*Fachhochschulen* / HAW) and dual study.** Germany has [200+ universities of applied sciences, enrolling close to four in ten of all students](https://www.mygermanuniversity.com/articles/university-of-applied-sciences-in-germany-uas); they deliver the great majority of the country's \~1,600 [*dual study* programmes](https://www.daad.de/en/studying-in-germany/universities/dual-studies/) (concentrated in engineering and business) that interleave degree study with paid workplace practice. This is the applied-engineering tier the UK's bimodal system mostly lacks.
5.  **Applied R&D (Fraunhofer).** At the top of the stack, the [Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft) — 76 institutes, \~32,000 staff, a \~€3.6bn budget, with contract research [explicitly aimed at the *Mittelstand*](https://www.fraunhofer.de/en/about-fraunhofer.html) — does the translational, process-innovation work that lets a mid-sized firm adopt technology it could never develop in-house. The UK's nearest analogue, the Catapult network and Sheffield's AMRC, is the subject of [universities_industrial_strategy_devolution.md](universities_industrial_strategy_devolution.md) §3 — proof-of-concept, the companion note argues, but not coverage.

### Why it holds together: governance and financing

This is the part the UK debate most often skips, and it is where "builds on institutions" earns its keep. Four load-bearing features:

-   **National occupational standards, co-produced by the social partners.** Each recognised occupation has a *training ordinance* (*Ausbildungsordnung*) developed under the Vocational Training Act ([*BBiG*](https://www.bibb.de/dokumente/pdf/bmbf_The_new_Vocational_Training_Act.pdf)) and the Crafts Code, through the [Federal Institute for Vocational Education and Training (BIBB)](https://www.bibb.de/en/77216.php) and [its tripartite Main Board](https://www.bibb.de/en/40.php) — employers, trade unions, the federal government and the *Länder* at one table. The standard is national and uniform; the certificate therefore means the same thing everywhere. This is the *Berufsprinzip* (occupational principle) made operational.
-   **The chambers as compulsory, self-administering public-law bodies.** Firms do not opt into the chambers; [membership is mandatory by statute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_German_Chambers_of_Industry_and_Commerce) (the IHK Act dates to 1956) and is financed by [member levies](https://www.ihk.de/rheinhessen/en/servicelabels/chamber-membership-4085630), which keeps the chambers financially and institutionally *independent of the government budget cycle*. They register every training contract, monitor quality, run the exams and award the *Meisterbrief*. This is the institutional-permanence point of §5 reappearing in the skills domain: the body that governs training cannot be defunded or reorganised at a minister's discretion.
-   **The *Länder* run the schools.** The *Berufsschule* side is a state competence, with framework curricula coordinated across states by the *Kultusministerkonferenz* — so the school half of the "dual" rests on the same constitutional foundation as the rest of *Land* education policy.
-   **Co-financing makes firm-level training rational.** [BIBB cost-benefit surveys](https://www.bibb.de/en/146543.php) put the *net* cost at roughly €6,500 per apprentice per year, with about two-thirds of gross costs recouped through the apprentice's own productive work, and firms bearing around three-quarters of total system costs (the public purse the rest). Employers train voluntarily and at scale because the economics work out — retention, saved recruitment, a known standard — and that *only* works because the occupation is standardised and the credential portable, so the firm is not training a worker it is bound to lose on unfavourable terms.

### The cultural layer: parity of esteem

The institutional stack is reinforced by a cultural one the UK conspicuously lacks. The [*Berufsprinzip*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-011-5204-4_5) treats a *Beruf* (occupation) as a holistic identity and the principal currency for entering the labour market, and the qualifications framework is built around [parity of esteem between vocational and academic routes](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0269094215589311) and *permeability* between them (the *Meister*-to-degree ladder above). In Britain the relationship between vocational qualification and employment is far looser, and the academic route carries more prestige — which is exactly why "more apprenticeships" as a UK policy slogan keeps under-delivering: the status problem is upstream of the programme design.

### How this maps onto the UK reforms — where they support, where they diverge

The UK is mid-reform, and the relevant instruments are catalogued in the two companion notes: **Skills England** (stood up [2 June 2025](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10365/), absorbing IfATE), the **Growth and Skills Levy** (replacing the apprenticeship levy from [April 2026](https://find-employer-schemes.education.gov.uk/interim/growth-and-skills-levy)), **T-Levels**, **Technical Excellence Colleges**, **Local Skills Improvement Plans**, the **devolved Adult Skills Fund** and university **degree apprenticeships**. Read against the German pipeline:

**Where the reforms support a Mittelstand reading:**

-   **Technical Excellence Colleges are sector-anchored in a near-Mittelstand way.** The [29 TECs](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/apply-to-become-a-technical-excellence-college) — starting with construction in 2025/26 and extending from April 2026 into defence, clean energy, digital and advanced manufacturing — are explicitly aligned to the IS-8 clusters this note's §4 already maps onto South Yorkshire and the North. They are the closest English move toward *occupation-specific, cluster-rooted* provision.
-   **LSIPs are a faint chamber echo.** Employer-led plans that shape college provision gesture, at low resolution, at the demand-articulation the German chambers do — though without statutory membership, levy funding, or exam-awarding power.
-   **The degree-apprenticeship + AMRC tier gestures at *Fachhochschule* + Fraunhofer.** As [universities_industrial_strategy_devolution.md](universities_industrial_strategy_devolution.md) (§2–3) argues, this is the part the UK has *proven* but not *scaled or regionalised*.

**Where they diverge — and where the gap is widest:**

-   **The trajectory is decline, not build-out — and skewed away from the young.** English apprenticeship starts dropped after the 2017 levy from a [2016/17 peak of 494,900](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn06113/) and have only partly recovered (\~350k a year), but the fall is concentrated exactly where a Mittelstand pipeline would need volume: under-25 starts down roughly a third, level-2 starts down \~72%, and starts in the most deprived neighbourhoods down \~47% (2017–2024), with [around half of all starts now going to over-25s](https://ifs.org.uk/articles/what-happened-youth-apprenticeships) and employer training spend down [billions in real terms since the levy](https://www.makeuk.org/news-and-events/news/calls-skills-investment-pledge-reverse-plummeting-apprenticeship-numbers). You cannot build a Mittelstand pipeline on a youth-apprenticeship base that has thinned at the bottom.
-   **The levy reforms raise the marginal cost of training at the SME end.** The [Growth and Skills Levy changes](https://www.grantthornton.co.uk/insights/apprenticeship-levy-reforms-what-employers-need-to-know-for-2026/) — funds expiring in 12 months instead of 24, employer co-investment rising from 5% to 25% once the balance is spent, the monthly top-up withdrawn, level-7 funding restricted to 16–21-year-olds — tighten terms precisely on the mid-market and smaller firms a Mittelstand strategy needs training (the under-25 full-funding carve-out for non-levy SMEs partly offsets this). Set against the German net-cost economics, English SMEs face a *rising and less predictable* training cost where German firms face a co-financed, chamber-certified, *standard* one.
-   **No occupational principle, no portable chamber certificate, no compulsory employer body.** English provision is contestable, frequently re-specified, and centrally redesigned (IfATE → Skills England in under a decade), rather than co-owned by durable social partners with their own funding base. This is the institutional content behind the universities note's phrase "a *missing middle in skills institutions*" mirroring the missing middle in firms.
-   **Governance is split the wrong way.** In Germany the whole pipeline is a *Land* competence wrapped around *national* occupational standards. In England skills are half-devolved (the Adult Skills Fund and LSIPs at MSA level) and half-centralised (the levy, the standards, T-Levels, Skills England) — securing neither the constitutional permanence of §5 nor the national-standard coherence of the *Ausbildungsordnung*.

### Don't idealise the model — it is itself under strain

Consistent with §2's caveat that the Mittelstand is "not uniformly healthy in 2026," the pipeline that feeds it is creaking too. Germany went into the 2025 apprenticeship year with [around 130,000 unfilled training places](https://www.dihk.de/en/trainee-shortage-and-economic-crisis-strain-the-apprenticeship-market-167224) and roughly every second firm unable to fill all its slots; there is a persistent applicant–vacancy *mismatch*, a documented [academisation drift](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1024258919898115) as more school-leavers choose university, and a [growing reliance on international applicants](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-germany-2025_39d62aed-en/full-report/addressing-skilled-labour-shortages_9edb78e6.html). The honest framing: the UK is trying to assemble parts of a model that is *itself* under demographic and academisation pressure — the target is moving, not static.

### The reading in one line

The German skills pipeline is load-bearing not because apprenticeships exist but because a stack of *durable, co-owned institutions* — compulsory chambers, social-partner occupational standards, *Land*-run schools, a permeable *Meister*→*Fachhochschule*→Fraunhofer ladder, and co-financing economics that make firm-level training rational — turns training into a portable, predictable investment. England is assembling many of the *parts* (Skills England, the Growth and Skills Levy, the IS-aligned Technical Excellence Colleges, LSIPs, the AMRC) but as centrally-set, frequently-reorganised *programmes* rather than entrenched institutions, and is doing so while apprenticeship volumes fall. The IS note's "place rhetoric without the substrate" and the universities note's "devolution can redeploy but not refinance" are the same gap seen through the skills lens: the UK can commission courses; it has not yet built the institution.

## 7. Where the Companies House work fits — a Yorkshire angle

The previous sections leave a clear gap: even if the constitutional / fiscal substrate isn't there, the *empirical* question of "*do we actually have* Mittelstand-shaped firms in the region, and where?" can be answered now. That's directly what the work in [companieshouseopen](/Users/danolner/Code/companieshouseopen) and the [SYMCA_YPERN_July2025.qmd](/Users/danolner/Code/RegionalEconomicTools/SYMCA_YPERN_July2025.qmd) pipelines builds toward.

### What we already have

The pipeline produces a [3.2M-row firm-level dataset](/Users/danolner/Code/companieshouseopen/claude/claudememory.md) for the live UK company population:

-   **Geography:** postcode → LA → ITL2 (so South Yorkshire, West Yorkshire, North Yorkshire are first-class objects, not residuals)
-   **Employees** (from iXBRL account extracts) and **dormancy status** — letting us strip out the shell-company noise that dominates raw CH counts
-   **Firm age** (incorporation date) — a partial but cheap proxy for the long-horizon / multi-generational property
-   **SIC at 2/3/5-digit + section** — coarse but usable for cluster identification
-   **ML/NLP bespoke-sector classification from website text** — the work on SetFit/BERT pipelines explicitly designed to get past SIC's well-known limits (the *Amazon-and-Facebook-are-"other-business-services"* problem)

### Why this matters for a Mittelstand frame

A British Mittelstand argument that stops at "we want more mid-sized firms" is fundamentally untargeted. The Companies House work makes it *firm-specific*:

1.  **Find the actual missing middle, regionally.** Filter to ITL2 = South/West/North Yorkshire, non-dormant, employees in (say) 50–500 — this is the cohort the [Northern Growth Strategy](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/northern-growth-strategy-case-for-change/northern-growth-strategy-next-steps) names as "central to the North's economic strength" but does not enumerate. We can.

2.  **Identify hidden-champion candidates.** Mittelstand firms are defined partly by niche dominance. SIC alone can't see that — a "Manufacture of other special-purpose machinery n.e.c." code hides everything from a world-leading minerals-processing-equipment firm to a workshop fabricator. The bespoke-sector ML pipeline can re-classify firms by what their websites actually describe, then we can ask: *in this region, which sub-sub-sectors have one or two locally-headquartered firms that are globally tradable?* That's the hidden-champion shortlist.

3.  **Proxy for the "ethos" features.** The German definition we can't directly measure (family ownership, generational continuity, local rootedness) has partial proxies in CH data: long-incorporated firms (age 25+ years), HQ postcode still in the founding region, low director turnover (a future enrichment via the PSC + officers files). None of these are conclusive individually; combined they shortlist plausible Mittelstand-shaped firms for qualitative follow-up.

4.  **Make supply-chain claims auditable.** The Northern Growth Strategy talks about mid-sized firms "underpinning supply chains across priority IS-8 sectors" — but at the moment that's an assertion, not an evidence base. Bespoke-sector classification + ITL2 geography + employee counts lets a Mayoral authority answer: *how many South Yorkshire firms are actually in the defence supply chain? In life sciences? In clean energy? At what scale? Owned where?* This is the empirical layer the strategy currently lacks.

5.  **Spot succession and ownership risk.** A region-specific worry that maps directly onto the German experience: the Mittelstand model is creaking partly because of *Nachfolge* (succession) gaps as founder generations retire. CH age + officers data can identify the at-risk cohort — long-established mid-sized firms whose directors are in their 60s/70s — which is exactly the cohort patient regional capital should be courting before they're sold to PE or to non-UK acquirers.

### What the Mayoral authority could actually do with this

Reading these capabilities against Riordan's brief and the Northern Growth Strategy's commitments:

-   **Populate the "community of high growth businesses"** the strategy commits to establishing — instead of self-nomination, build the candidate list from the data. The CH firm-level dataset gives Mayoral authorities a defensible, reproducible long-list.
-   **Inform NPIF II targeting.** The [British Business Bank's Northern Powerhouse Investment Fund II](https://www.british-business-bank.co.uk/news-and-events/news/how-npif-ii-driving-growth-across-north-two-years) is the closest UK analogue to *Landesbank* patient capital. Its effectiveness depends on knowing *which* mid-sized firms in the region are growth-capable, succession-vulnerable, or exporting from a niche. The data we have can build that pipeline.
-   **Make the "visible regional leader" case data-backed.** The 90% mid-market survey finding cited earlier wants regional advocacy; advocates with a firm-level evidence base for what's actually in their region (and what's at risk) are more credible than ones citing only top-line GVA.
-   **Sector-specific skills investment.** Devolved adult skills budgets become much sharper if you know which mid-sized employers in your patch are in which bespoke sub-sector, and what their headcount trajectory looks like — instead of allocating against broad SIC averages.
-   **Track foreign acquisition / outsourcing.** A specific failure mode of the British mid-market (per the [Brittelstand critique](https://www.enterpriseresearch.ac.uk/the-mittelstand-v-silicon-valley-which-model-of-entrepreneurship-is-most-relevant-for-post-brexit-britain/)) is firms being sold off or moving production overseas. CH ownership-change events are visible in the data; this is monitorable.

### Directions this could take us

In rough order of effort:

1.  **A Yorkshire mid-market dashboard.** Filter, count, map: by ITL2, by SIC section, by bespoke sector, by firm age, by employee band. The cleanest first deliverable, useful immediately for SYMCA / WYCA / NYCC business boards.
2.  **A "hidden champion candidates" shortlist.** Combine bespoke-sector classification + small population in the sub-sector + regional concentration + 25+ years incorporated + steady or growing headcount. Qualitative follow-up needed but the data narrows the field to dozens, not thousands.
3.  **A succession-risk register.** Long-established mid-sized firms with director age signals (when officers data is joined in). A genuinely novel policy artefact — nobody else has this.
4.  **Supply-chain depth analysis per IS-8 sector.** For defence / life sciences / clean energy in the North, build the firm-level supply pyramid using bespoke classification, then test against the Northern Growth Strategy's claims.
5.  **Longitudinal tracking.** CH live list + account extracts re-run quarterly gives a region-specific firm dynamics measure — entries, exits, employment changes, ownership changes — at sub-regional resolution. This is the empirical infrastructure a serious regional industrial strategy actually needs and currently doesn't have.

The honest framing: the Companies House work cannot *create* a Mittelstand. The constitutional and institutional gaps identified in §5 are still gaps. But it can give a Mayoral authority, and Riordan's office, the firm-level evidence base for *targeted* mid-market policy — which is the difference between "British Mittelstand" as rhetoric and as a concrete, place-specific industrial strategy. The free-floating mid-sized firms exist in Yorkshire; we can see them; the question is what the policy layer chooses to do once it can.

## 8. The reading-in-one-line

The Northern Growth Strategy is the closest UK national policy has come to a Mittelstand-flavoured industrial strategy — mid-market firms, supply-chain resilience, place-based devolution, patient capital — but it is doing so without (yet) touching the institutional layer (vocational training, regional banking, ownership norms) that makes the German model load-bearing. Riordan's job, read sympathetically, is to start building that layer through devolution and Treasury influence; read sceptically, it's a 15-month sticking plaster on a 30-year institutional gap.

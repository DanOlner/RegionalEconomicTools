# The UK's Modern Industrial Strategy: what's happened since launch

*Compiled June 2026 — roughly one year on from publication. Notes on developments since the strategy and its eight focus sectors were defined, its impact (or not) on Local Growth Plans, and how it has fed into the Northern Growth Strategy. Several figures below come from government quarterly updates and secondary summaries; primary documents are linked in Sources.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## TL;DR

-   **The strategy** — *The UK's Modern Industrial Strategy* — was published **23 June 2025** as a 10-year plan, building on the Oct 2024 "Invest 2035" green paper. It defined **eight growth-driving sectors (the "IS-8")**, each with its own Sector Plan, plus supporting "foundational" sectors.
-   **Since then**, the government has run on two tracks: (1) **sector delivery** — quarterly updates, big investment announcements (Nissan, SMR at Wylfa, DRIVE35, data centres, Universal Studios), energy-cost relief, R&D and skills money; and (2) **place** — a suite of funds and the push to get every mayor to publish a **Local Growth Plan (LGP)**.
-   **Impact on Local Growth Plans:** real but asymmetric. LGPs are explicitly framed as the local delivery vehicle for the IS, shared priorities were agreed with mayors in **April 2025**, and several 10-year LGPs have now been published (Liverpool City Region, East Midlands, North East, Cambridgeshire & Peterborough, West Yorkshire). But critics argue the relationship is top-down — places implement centrally-set sector priorities rather than shape them.
-   **Northern Growth Strategy:** yes, the IS has fed in directly. The NGS **"Case for change" (Jan 2026)** and **"Next Steps" (19 March 2026)** are built around backing **IS-8 clusters where the North excels** (Leeds/Manchester financial & professional services, a North-East/Tees/Humber clean-energy "supercluster", South Yorkshire advanced manufacturing & defence), wrapped around the Northern Powerhouse Rail spine and city-centre investment. A consultation runs to **31 July 2026**, with a further update due in **autumn 2026**.
-   **Verdict so far:** business confidence has ticked up, but independent analysis (Institute for Government; a *Regional Studies* critical review; RSA Regions) is sceptical that a strategy this centralised can really be "place-based", and warns the hard part — cross-departmental delivery without much new money — is still ahead.

## 1. Recap: what was launched

In June 2025 the government published [**The UK's Modern Industrial Strategy**](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/the-uks-modern-industrial-strategy-2025) — a 10-year plan to "make Britain the best place to start and grow a business". It followed the [**Invest 2035 green paper**](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/invest-2035-the-uks-modern-industrial-strategy/invest-2035-the-uks-modern-industrial-strategy) (Oct 2024) and was accompanied by individual **Sector Plans** for each of the eight growth-driving sectors.

**The IS-8 sectors:**

1.  Advanced manufacturing
2.  Clean energy industries
3.  Creative industries
4.  Defence
5.  Digital and technologies
6.  Financial services
7.  Life sciences
8.  Professional and business services

Within these, the strategy named **30+ "frontier industries"** and a set of **"foundational" sectors** that underpin the eight. Headline commitments included: R&D spending rising to **£22.6bn/year by 2029-30** (incl. £2bn+ for AI, £2.8bn for advanced manufacturing over ten years); an extra **£1.2bn/year for skills by 2028-29**; and lifting **British Business Bank** financial capacity to **£25.6bn**.

For background and the long policy history, see the [**House of Commons Library briefing on industrial strategy**](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-7682/) and its companion on [**how much the IS sectors contribute to UK countries and regions**](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10439/).

## 2. What's changed since — sector delivery

The government now publishes [**quarterly Industrial Strategy updates**](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/the-uks-modern-industrial-strategy-2025) (Jul–Sep 2025, Oct–Dec 2025). The [**Oct–Dec 2025 update**](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/industrial-strategy-quarterly-update-october-to-december-2025/industrial-strategy-quarterly-update-october-to-december-2025-web-version) reported **£79bn+ of IS-8 investment commitments** and 50,000+ jobs in the quarter. Highlights by sector:

-   **Advanced manufacturing** — DRIVE35 zero-emission vehicle programme expanded to **£4bn to 2035**; **Nissan £450m** for next-gen Leaf in Sunderland (\~6,000 jobs).
-   **Clean energy** — **Wylfa (Anglesey)** chosen as the UK's first Small Modular Reactor site (£2.5bn, \~3,000 construction jobs); **Clean Energy Jobs Plan** (claiming 400,000 jobs by 2030); £1bn GB Energy supply-chain fund.
-   **Creative industries** — Discover! Creative Careers Month (80,000 young people); inward film/TV production wins; £369m sector R&D.
-   **Defence** — new munitions/energetics sites; "Back British" procurement consultation; first National Armaments Director appointed.
-   **Digital & technologies** — \~£4bn research/innovation funding; **AI Growth Lab** regulatory sandbox; £250m to expand researcher compute access.
-   **Financial services** — Office for Investment "concierge"; FCA Scale-Up Unit; **PISCES** private-company stock market approved.
-   **Life sciences** — UK–US pharma deal (zero tariffs on exports); £50m Mental Health Goals programme.
-   **Professional & business services** — UK–Switzerland services mobility deal extended to 2029; English Law Promotion Panel; PropTech coalition.

**Energy costs** — a recurring industry ask — are being addressed via relief of **up to 90% on electricity network charges from April 2026** for energy-intensive users (the British Industry Supercharger / network-charging compensation route), alongside the planned **British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme** to cut electricity costs for eligible firms.

On the trade-body view, manufacturers report improved sentiment — [**SOCI/Make UK**](https://www.soci.org/news/2026/1/industrial-strategy-has-boosted-business-confidence-but-more-work-is-needed) found nearly two-thirds of manufacturers think 2026's opportunities outweigh the risks, crediting the IS with "tipping the balance in favour of confidence" — but flag that **detail and implementation** are still lacking.

## 3. Impact on Local Growth Plans

This is where the IS most directly touches the regional agenda — and the picture is real but uneven.

**The mechanism.** Every Mayoral Strategic Authority (MSA) — Mayoral Combined Authorities, Combined County Authorities and the GLA — is expected to publish a **10-year [Local Growth Plan](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/local-growth-plans-england/guidance-for-mayoral-strategic-authorities-on-developing-local-growth-plans)** setting out where it will focus devolved powers and funding to "help deliver the Industrial Strategy" by building on local economic strengths. Crucially, **each IS-8 Sector Plan states how central government will work with MSAs and "join up" with LGPs** — so the two are designed to interlock.

**The timeline.** - **April 2025** — the Deputy Prime Minister wrote to mayors confirming [**collectively agreed shared LGP priorities**](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/local-growth-plans-england/agreed-shared-local-growth-plan-priorities). - Through **2025–26**, LGPs began publishing. By the Oct–Dec 2025 quarter, **10-year plans were out for Liverpool City Region, East Midlands, North East, and Cambridgeshire & Peterborough**; [**West Yorkshire's LGP 2025–2035**](https://www.westyorks-ca.gov.uk/growing-the-economy/economic-strategy/local-growth-plan/) ("Corridors of Opportunity") has also been published. - **Funding plumbing** now flows through **multi-year Integrated Settlements** — **at least £13bn from 2026-27 to 2029-30** across seven MSAs (Greater Manchester, West Midlands, Liverpool City Region, West Yorkshire, North East, South Yorkshire, GLA).

**Place-based funds introduced alongside the IS:** - **£600m Strategic Sites Accelerator** (from 2025/26) for investment-ready land. - **£500m [Local Innovation Partnerships Fund](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/regional-leaders-to-get-more-power-over-innovation-funding-to-drive-local-jobs-and-growth)** for high-potential clusters (first 10 places confirmed; further competition launched). - **£500m Mayoral Recyclable/Revolving Growth Fund**, aimed especially at the North and Midlands.

**The catch.** Local-government and analyst commentary stresses the **tension between a nationally-set sector strategy and locally-owned growth plans** — see the [**LGA briefing on the IS**](https://www.local.gov.uk/parliament/briefings-and-responses/uks-modern-industrial-strategy-local-authority-policy), techUK's [**Local Growth Plans tracker**](https://www.techuk.org/resource/local-growth-plans.html), and *The MJ*'s [**"Balancing UK industrial strategy with Local Growth Plans"**](https://www.themj.co.uk/balancing-uk-industrial-strategy-local-growth-plans). The risk repeatedly flagged: LGPs end up *implementing* centrally-defined IS-8 priorities rather than genuinely shaping them, and areas outside the eight sectors feel left behind.

## 4. Has it fed into the Northern Growth Strategy? — Yes, directly

The [**Northern Growth Strategy**](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/northern-growth-strategy) is the clearest example of the IS being scaled up to a pan-regional level.

-   [**"Case for change"**](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/northern-growth-strategy-case-for-change/northern-growth-strategy-case-for-change) — published **January 2026**.
-   [**"Next Steps"**](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/northern-growth-strategy-case-for-change/northern-growth-strategy-next-steps) — laid in Parliament **19 March 2026** ([written statement](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-03-19/hcws1423); [full PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69bbbad2e2678e3266bbe5ad/Northern_Growth_Strategy_Next_Steps.pdf)).

**How the IS feeds in.** The NGS is explicitly built to "**back Industrial Strategy clusters where they excel**". Its economic prize: lifting the five most populous northern MSAs to national-average productivity could add **\~£40bn/year in GVA** and \~£15bn/year in fiscal revenues. It leans on **all eight IS-8 sectors** where the North has advantage, naming clusters such as:

-   **West Yorkshire** — financial & professional services, the **"Northern Square Mile"** in Leeds.
-   **North East / Tees Valley / York & North Yorkshire / Humber** — a **clean-energy "supercluster"** (offshore wind, hydrogen, CCUS).
-   **South Yorkshire** — **advanced manufacturing and defence**, via a **£50m Defence Growth Deal**.
-   **Greater Manchester / Liverpool** — life sciences, advanced materials, AI/cyber, MediaCity.

**Wrapped around infrastructure and devolution:** the **Northern Growth Corridor** (York–Liverpool across the Pennines), **Northern Powerhouse Rail** as the spine, **£1.7bn City Investment Funds** (incl. \~£620m new grants — see [**Chancellor backs North with £1.7bn**](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/chancellor-backs-north-with-17bn-for-city-centre-projects)), and deeper integrated settlements for MSAs. **Tom Riordan** was appointed Northern Growth Corridor envoy.

**Relationship to LGPs:** the NGS positions itself as **"complementing" not directing** MSA Local Growth Plans — pan-regional collaboration on top of locally-owned plans, exemplified by the **White Rose Agreement** between Yorkshire mayors.

**Next steps:** the "invitation for views" (six questions on transport, densification, financing, skills, participation, culture) **closes 31 July 2026**, with a further NGS update intended for **autumn 2026**.

For an independent regional take, see Yorkshire Universities' [**"The Northern Growth Strategy: a pragmatic approach to pan-regional collaboration?"**](https://yorkshireuniversities.ac.uk/2026/01/19/the-northern-growth-strategy-a-pragmatic-approach-to-pan-regional-collaboration/) (Dr Peter O'Brien) — broadly welcoming, but pointedly notes the long line of predecessors (the Northern Way, Northern Powerhouse, Northern Futures) without a clear account of why this attempt succeeds where they didn't.

## 5. What else has happened — governance, summits, other geographies

-   **Regional investment summits** — a **Birmingham summit** drew **£10bn+** in commitments, including £400m government support for a £4bn Birmingham Knowledge Quarter; a **Wales summit** confirmed \~£1.4bn private investment.
-   **AI Growth Zones** announced (incl. North & South Wales), alongside the **Oxford–Cambridge Growth Corridor** package.
-   **Delivery architecture** — Sector Plans carry Senior Responsible Officers and monitoring commitments; the strategy is steered with input from an Industrial Strategy Advisory Council, and progress is tracked via the quarterly updates.
-   **Parliamentary scrutiny** — the [**Business and Trade Committee**](https://committees.parliament.uk/publications/49497/documents/263605/default/) has been examining the IS and the government's response.

## 6. Critical reception — is it really "place-based"?

This is the sharpest debate, and the most relevant to regional-economics work.

-   **Institute for Government — ["Delivering the new industrial strategy will be a huge test"](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/comment/industrial-strategy-white-paper):** welcomes the clearer focus and monitoring, but warns the central risk is **unprecedented cross-departmental coordination** (in some sector plans only \~1/3 of actions sit with the business department), a proliferation of councils/panels that could become "stakeholder handling", hard-to-measure supply-side effects, and the underlying bet that **consistency alone — without major new funding — convinces business to invest**.
-   [**A Critical Review of the UK's Modern Industrial Strategy: Lessons for "Place-Based" Policy**](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00343404.2025.2597466) (*Regional Studies*, Vol 60 No 1; [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/a-critical-review-of-the-uks-modern-industrial-strategy-lessons-f/)): argues the strategy's place-based claim **misses the mark**, because you cannot run genuinely place-based policy inside a **highly centralised economy with underpowered, fragmented regional governance**.
-   **RSA Regions — ["Is the UK's Modern Industrial Strategy really Place-Based?"](https://regions.regionalstudies.org/ezine/article/issue-24-uk-industrial-strategy-place-based/):** calls it **overly centralised, overly technocratic**, and warns the IS-8's high-tech focus **marginalises the "everyday/foundational economy"** (SMEs, traditional manufacturing, logistics, care, tourism, local services) that dominates many regions. Recommends a **genuinely multi-level** model — national missions, real regional authority, stronger local institutional capacity and stable funding, judged on long-term outcomes rather than headline announcements.

**Net:** a year in, the IS has produced a steady drumbeat of investment announcements and a coherent local-delivery scaffolding (shared priorities → Local Growth Plans → integrated settlements → the Northern Growth Strategy). The open questions are whether central government can actually deliver across departments, whether places get real agency rather than implementation duties, and whether anywhere outside the eight sectors and a handful of corridors sees the benefit.

## 7. Reflection: overlap with the Mittelstand / devolution analysis

A companion note in this folder — [mittelstand.md](mittelstand.md) — works the same terrain (the Northern Growth Strategy, the IS-8 clusters, Tom Riordan's envoy role, devolution) but from a different starting point: the German **Mittelstand** and whether English devolution could ever be *Land*-like. The two are best read together. Here is where they reinforce each other and where they pull apart.

### Where they converge

1.  **The same diagnosis by two routes — "place rhetoric without the substrate."** Section 6 above reports the IS critics (RSA Regions, the *Regional Studies* review, the IfG) arguing the strategy cannot be genuinely place-based inside a highly centralised state with weak, fragmented regional governance. The Mittelstand note reaches an identical verdict from the comparative angle: you cannot grow Mittelstand-shaped firms without the *Land*-level institutional stack (vocational training, regional banks, multi-decade sub-national industrial policy). The IS critique says *the place-based claim is hollow*; the Mittelstand note names **precisely which institutions are missing** that would make it real. Same argument — the Mittelstand frame is just the more specific one.
2.  **Shared objects.** Both lean on the same March 2026 NGS *Next Steps* document, the same IS-8 clusters (South Yorkshire defence, the NE/Tees/Humber clean-energy supercluster, life sciences), the same integrated settlements, and the same person — **Tom Riordan**, who appears here only as "Northern Growth Corridor envoy" but is the central character of the Mittelstand note.
3.  **Finance and skills instruments, reframed.** The funds catalogued above (British Business Bank capacity, the Local Innovation Partnerships Fund, the Mayoral Recyclable Growth Fund, devolved skills budgets) are exactly the instruments the Mittelstand note reads as thin UK analogues to *Sparkassen*/*Landesbanken* patient capital and the dual training system. Same plumbing; the Mittelstand note judges it against the German benchmark and finds it under-scaled.

### Where they diverge — and what each document misses

1.  **Firm structure vs. sector-and-place.** This document's lens is *sectors and geographies*; it has almost nothing on firm size, ownership, or governance. The Mittelstand note's lens is the *mid-market firm* — the "missing middle", family ownership, succession, hidden champions. The NGS itself partly bridges this (it names mid-sized firms as central to northern supply chains), which the sector-and-place reading above under-weights.
2.  **The foundational-economy critique points the *opposite way* to the Mittelstand argument.** Both attack the IS-8's narrowness, but from opposite ends. RSA Regions wants the strategy to stop ignoring the *low-productivity everyday economy* (care, logistics, local services); the Mittelstand argument wants it to build up *globally-tradable niche manufacturers*. These are different — even rival — prescriptions for "what the IS-8 leaves out", and shouldn't be collapsed into a single "it's too narrow" complaint.
3.  **Constitutional depth.** The place-based critique here stops at "centralised state, fragmented governance." The Mittelstand note goes much further — federalism vs. statutory devolution, constitutional entrenchment, the *Bundesrat*, fiscal equalisation, the \~5%-of-tax-collected-locally gap. The IS analysis doesn't touch the constitutional question at all, which is arguably where the real ceiling on place-based policy sits.
4.  **Ownership, ethos and succession — wholly absent here.** Family ownership, multi-generational horizons, succession risk, the tax treatment of patient family capital: none appear in the IS or its critiques, yet they are the *defining* features of the Mittelstand. A sector strategy and a firm-governance strategy are simply different animals, and the IS is firmly the former.
5.  **Top-down policy vs. bottom-up evidence.** Everything above is read off published strategy and commentary. The Mittelstand note's §7 adds something neither the IS nor its critics have: a *firm-level empirical base* (the Companies House work) to actually locate a region's mid-market and hidden-champion candidates — the layer that could turn "back IS-8 clusters where the North excels" from assertion into a targeted, auditable list.
6.  **Corridor vs. distributed cluster.** This document describes the Northern Growth *Corridor* (York–Liverpool, on the Oxford–Cambridge analogy) approvingly. The Mittelstand note flags that a corridor/agglomeration geography pulls against the distributed small- and mid-town cluster geography the German model relies on — a tension the sector reading here doesn't surface. **This is the single biggest geographic divergence between the two strategies, and §8 below unpacks it in detail.**

### One-line synthesis

Both documents land in the same place: the Industrial Strategy and the Northern Growth Strategy have adopted the *language* of place (and, in the NGS, of mid-market supply chains) without yet building the institutional substrate that would make either a genuinely place-based or a genuinely Mittelstand-style strategy. This document shows that gap as the unresolved tension in the strategy's own critical reception; [mittelstand.md](mittelstand.md) names the specific German institutions whose absence defines it — and points at the firm-level data work that could at least make the mid-market ambition concrete.

## 8. Geography deep-dive: agglomeration-and-corridor vs. distributed-cluster growth

The Mittelstand note ([mittelstand.md](mittelstand.md), §4) ends one of its sharpest observations with the line: *"the Mittelstand story is fundamentally a **distributed** small-town and mid-town cluster story"*, set against the Northern Growth Strategy's *"corridor/agglomeration story."* This is worth pulling out on its own, because it isn't a difference of emphasis — the two strategies rest on **different theories of where productivity actually comes from**, and those theories point investment, transport and institutions in genuinely different directions.

### Two models of where growth comes from

**Model A — agglomeration and corridors (the IS/NGS bet).** The intellectual root is New Economic Geography: productivity rises with **density and proximity** — thick labour markets, knowledge spillovers, shared suppliers, the benefits of firms and workers being near each other. The policy implication is to **grow and densify big cities and connect them** so they behave like one larger market. The NGS is explicitly this: its stated aims are to "grow and densify northern cities", to run **Northern Powerhouse Rail** as the connective spine, and to open up the **Northern Growth Corridor** (York–Liverpool), rhetorically modelled on the [Oxford–Cambridge Arc](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/northern-growth-strategy-case-for-change/northern-growth-strategy-next-steps). Behind it sits a specific diagnosis: northern cities are **too small and too disconnected** relative to their potential, and the UK is unusually [monocentric](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00343404.2019.1619928) — dominated by London's primacy in a way Germany is not. The headline £40bn prize is framed around the **five most populous** Mayoral Strategic Authorities reaching national-average productivity. The whole construct is a bet on building northern agglomeration to counterweight London.

**Model B — distributed / polycentric clusters (the Mittelstand pattern).** Germany's [hidden champions](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362198831_Hidden_Champions_and_the_Development_of_Regions) are disproportionately located **outside the big cities** — roughly **one in five of \~1,700 sit outside densely populated areas**, with long-established family manufacturers embedded in small towns across [Baden-Württemberg, Bavaria, NRW and Hesse](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11301-021-00253-6). The [Leibniz Institute work on small towns in peripheral regions](https://leibniz-ifl.de/en/home/research/project/hidden-champions-stabilisation-and-development-factors-of-small-towns-in-peripheral-regions) finds these firms measurably *stabilise* the towns that host them (human capital, demographics, tax base). This geography is only possible because Germany has a **polycentric urban system** (no primate city) and **Land-level institutions in every region** — training, *Sparkassen*, applied research. Growth is **spread across many small and mid-sized towns**, not concentrated in metro cores.

### Why the two pull in different directions

1.  **Where the money lands.** Agglomeration directs investment into **city centres** (densification, CBD regeneration, station-led development); the distributed model spreads it across **dispersed towns**.
2.  **What transport is for.** The NGS logic is **inter-city trunk rail** binding big nodes into one labour market — NPR speeds Leeds–Manchester. The distributed logic prizes **local connectivity** binding towns to their hinterland; fast inter-city rail does comparatively little for Barnsley, Grimsby, Burnley or Scarborough.
3.  **The "left-behind towns" problem.** This is the politically loaded one. An agglomeration strategy can **bypass exactly the towns** whose discontent drove the "levelling up" agenda in the first place — McCann's ["geography of discontent"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00343404.2019.1619928). Critics of the earlier Northern Powerhouse ([IPPR's *City systems*](https://www.ippr.org/articles/city-systems), the [IEA](https://iea.org.uk/blog/re-heating-regional-policy-flaws-in-the-%E2%80%98northern-powerhouse%E2%80%99), and the ["Powerhouse of cards?"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00343404.2016.1196289) analysis) warned that a city-centric, implicitly trickle-down model did not reach surrounding towns and risked **worsening inequality *within* the North**. IPPR's prescription was explicitly to stop framing it as "cities versus towns" and instead manage the *relationships* between cities, towns and rural areas.
4.  **The evidence is not settled.** The empirical case for agglomeration benefits in UK core cities is **mixed at best** — productivity and employment in many core cities have run *below* national rates — so Model A is a genuine bet, not a sure thing.

### Why Germany can be distributed and the UK can't simply choose to be

The crucial point is that the UK **cannot just pick** the distributed model, because the two preconditions are missing:

-   **Urban structure.** Britain's monocentric, London-dominated settlement pattern is centuries deep; Germany's polycentricity is structural. You cannot legislate a polycentric urban system into existence in a 10-year plan.
-   **Institutional substrate.** The distributed German model works because the *Land*-level institutions (vocational training, regional banks, Fraunhofer-style applied research) exist in **every** region and town, not just the metros — the very stack the Mittelstand note's §5 shows England lacks.

Read sympathetically, this makes the NGS's agglomeration bet a **rational response to monocentricity**: the UK can't conjure dispersed hidden champions out of thin institutions, but it might plausibly build a small number of northern agglomerations big enough to matter. Read sceptically, it means the strategy is **doubling down on the city-core geography** at precisely the moment its own political mandate comes from the towns.

### Where the NGS hedges — it isn't *purely* agglomeration

The contrast shouldn't be overdrawn. The NGS also commits to "**back IS clusters where they excel**", and clusters need not sit in city cores — advanced manufacturing in Rotherham/Barnsley, clean energy on the Humber and Teesside, defence supply chains in South Yorkshire are town- and coast-based, not Leeds-CBD phenomena. The strategy's **mid-market / supply-chain language** (the part the Mittelstand note seizes on) points toward a more distributed reading than its **NPR-plus-densification headline** does. The honest summary: the NGS contains *both* instincts, but its flagship instruments — Northern Powerhouse Rail, city densification, the Growth Corridor — are agglomeration-first, while the distributed-cluster ambition is real but under-resourced and under-specified.

### Bottom line

The Industrial Strategy and the Northern Growth Strategy are, at their core, an **agglomeration-and-connectivity bet**: make northern cities bigger, denser and better-connected to rival London's pull. The Mittelstand is a **distributed-town** model that depends on a polycentric urban system and town-level institutions the UK doesn't have. These are competing geographies, not complementary ones — and the risk the regional-economics literature keeps flagging is that an agglomeration strategy can lift the North's *aggregate* GVA (the £40bn headline) while leaving its **towns** — the places the politics is actually about — relatively further behind. A genuinely Mittelstand-flavoured strategy would require the opposite geographic instinct *and* the distributed institutional capacity to support it, which is exactly what remains absent.

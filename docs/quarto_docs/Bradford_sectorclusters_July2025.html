CONNECTION TO OTHER SECTOR STRATEGIES

Including a detailed link to the UK Industrial Stategy

UK Industrial Strategy

Overview

The UK Industrial Strategy was released in 2025, with accompanying plans for 6 out of the 8 target “IS-8” sectors. It also has a sector definition list that provides SIC codes being targeted - top level sectors and ‘frontier’ subsectors (a few of which overlap; most do not). These range in specificity between 2 digit and 5 digit SIC codes.

As that webpage says, most of these SIC codes cannot capture the full range of possible firms the strategy aims to cover, and it also contains entire sections that SIC codes can’t cover at all - clean energy and certain digital sectors in particular. However, it does allow an analysis of which sectors are definitely within those industrial strategy categories. This section gathers job details for those specific sectors - a distinct range of types and breadth - and compares Bradford to the rest of the UK where possible, to see which sectors align with the industrial strategy and other analysis above. This is done for both BRES and Companies House job counts, to pick up on different job types.

This document has a data section (“UK INDUSTRIAL STRATEGY LINKAGE”) that pulls out Bradford sectors in this ‘IndStrat’ list - giving job counts, LQs and change over time from both BRES and Companies House data.

The table below lists the IS-8 broad sector headings. In the IndStrat plots, the abbreviations for these IS-8 headings appear before the sector name. Clean energy - as mentioned above - has no SIC codes and so can’t currently be matched. There are no matching defence sectors or firms in Bradford.

Points from the IndStrat LQ plots for Bradford

The IndStrat LQ plots pick out for Bradford which IS-sectors are more concentrated job-wise, what their trajectory is and what the job counts are. This section can be read in conjunction with the more detailed data/sector analysis below, from which it draws.

There are three sectors in particular identified below as being important to Bradford but facing productivity challenges that could benefit from support flowing from the industrial strategy: chemical production, head offices / management consultancy and architect/engineering/tech testing services. See the analysis below for a description of the history of these sectors.

  • Bradford has both manufacturing and service strengths that marry with the industrial strategy.
  • From those examined below, Bradford’s IS-8 overlaps are:
    • PBS: across a number of sectors, including legal services, accounting, head offices and management consultancy.
    • Finance: as well as banks, Companies House accounts picks out a very large number of holding companies in the finance category.
    • Advanced manufacturing: the machinery / equipment not elsewhere classified SIC covers a lot of advanced firms including space sectors, and is 3000 jobs in BRES. While architect/engineering/tech testing is technically PBS, it has important connections to production and faces challenges. Chemical production has already been mentioned as key to the industrial strategy but a nationally struggling sector.
    • Digital: electronic instuments/navigation isn’t a large number of jobs relatively, but it is likely a part of the larger advanced manufacturing / digital tech crossover.

West Yorkshire Local Growth Plan

The LGP’s “priority one” is unlocking growth in sectors.

That page has a detailed outline of subsectors across West Yorkshire, and it argues for building on those with the largest job presence, including textiles and food manufacture, as well as chemicals. Each of these sectors are identified below as facing long-standing productivity challenges; here, other advanced manufacturing sectors are also highlighted as vital for Bradford (especially metals and machinery/transport manufacture - the WY LGP cites space tech too, a lot of which will be in these categories). Chemical production is a focus of the UK Industrial Strategy - including as part of its “foundational inputs” category - and so could potentially benefit the most, out of all high-job-count / low-output sectors listed here.

The LGP also singles out financial and professional services as vital, and for Bradford, the analysis below absolutely bears this out - though again, with some high growth slope sectors facing productivity issues (head offices), other Bradford strengths are in a much stronger position (legal/accounting) while Bradford finance is productive but possibly static (though Companies House data suggests a dynamic finance sector).

The high growth cluster analysis in the LGP puts “engineering … at the heart of these opportunities as a driver and foundation of growth.” As mentioned, engineering is an historic Bradford strength that is facing a long term productivity issue; understanding this better could help support this ‘cluster action’ approach.

West Yorkshire LSIP (Local Skills Improvement Plan) published 2023

Page 4 of the West Yorkshire LSIP says it aims “ro enable employers and individuals to access the technical skills they need to support growth, transformation and increased productivity in our key sectors, defined as:”

  • Health and Social Care
  • Engineering and Advanced Manufacturing
  • Financial and Professional Services
  • Low Carbon
  • Digital and Technology
  • Creative Industries
  • Education
  • Construction
  • Transport and Logistics

The analysis below digs further into engineering, advanced manufacturing and financial and professional services - all Bradford sector strengths, with differing productivity challenges, though these are especially notable in engineering.

Other sectors listed in LSIP could go into two categories:

  1. Broad sectors where, with the data here, it is difficult to shed much light on productivity issues. Health and social care faces the same issues as other mainly-public sectors in the data - they have clearly grown to be the largest or one of the largest broad sectors, but public sector productivity analysis is difficult (partly because of how ONS calculate it at a regional level - they just use job count times regional job average to estimate GVA). This in no way negates the need to improve health and social care skills, it just makes it hard to analyse productivity data.
    • Companies House data does show that both health and social care have a very large private sector presence in Bradford (Figure 9, Figure 10 and plots from ?@fig-indivfirmsSY on to identify specific firms), so these could well benefit / be benefiting from from LSIP-related support.
  2. Other sectors have not been picked out below for specific analysis, but are high job count with national-level productivity issues, and often not highly tradeable beyond Bradford. See for example the 2D GVA vs job plots for transport (Figure 37) and construction (Figure 36).

HEADLINES FROM THE DATA ANALYSIS

The data

Three main data sources are used here (and one other smaller ONS source for the hourly productivity plots).

The main three data sources are:

  • ONS regional GVA data at ITL3 geographies, up to 2023 (the most recent available data).
  • BRES (Business Register and Employment Survey) data for job counts by sector and place, again up to 2023 for the latest.
  • Data extracted from Companies House accounts for individual firms in the last year, between June 2024 and end of June 2025. This data only contains firms with a registered address in Bradford, so it misses all firms with ownership registered elsewhere (e.g. this includes Produmax, registered in Manchester). However, it can provide a highly granular and up-to-date picture of sector economies, including employment change in the last year.

For some analyses, regional GVA and BRES jobs data are linked. To do this, BRES jobs are binned into the smaller list of 44 SIC groupings that the ONS uses for the GVA data, so e.g. GVA per full time job can be examined. Where possible, the most granular sector breakdowns are used.

Lastly, the UK government’s Industrial Strategy sector definition list is used to examine how Bradford’s economy meshes with that strategy.

All code for this analysis is available here, and here for processing Companies House accounts.

Service sectors

Strongest service sectors

  • The head offices and management consultancy sector (SIC 70, 2.3% of FT jobs, 2.7% of GVA; Figure 4) jumps out in several of the plots below (looking at 5 digit job counts for this sector, this is mostly ‘head office activities’ in Bradford). While not a very productivity sector overall, it has the strongest GVA and jobs growth slopes of any sector, averaging 15% GVA and 16.7% GVA per FT job growth from 2015 to 2023 (Figure 14), statistically separable from most other places, and becoming even stronger in more recent years (Figure 16). Plotting its GVA percent change (2015/17 to 2021/23 averages) against the same for jobs (Figure 22) shows (in the lighter diagonal corner) how its productivity gains have come from GVA growth mostly, but including a jobs increases, and that Bradford’s trajectory has been one of the strongest, percentage-wise. Figure 2 (last panel) shows output per worker for the sector has been historically low everywhere, but Bradford has seen a large jump in trend since COVID, and it is now some way above the average for other ITL3 zones in the data (50K per FT versus 36).
    • Location quotients for the sector show a contrast (Figure 4): GVA continuing to increase its concentration, but more recently Bradford’s jobs in this sector being less concentrated in Bradford than the GB average (though the jobs LQ has hovered between more and less over time). The last year’s Companies House (CH) data, though, sees the sector growing its job count more than any other in Bradford, and more than other West Yorkshire authorities for firms with 10+ employees (Figure 9) - only a small number of firms in CH (8 showing a 14.2% employee increase overall) but indicative of sector growth.
  • Finance should possibly be at the top of this list, as it is 5.4% of Bradford’s economy and 3.7% of its full time workforce (4900 jobs in BRES; Figure 4). However, it doesn’t stand out in comparison to other Bradford sectors or the rest of GB. For example, in its 2D GVA vs jobs percent change plot (Figure 30), while its productivity has risen, it’s only a little - though note, it is at least in the more positive half in that plot, compared to many other places with productivity drops (in the grey top left diagonal).
    • The ‘finance’ SIC category in the GVA data covers SICs 64 to 66 (finance, auxiliaries and insurance) - looking deeper into the BRES 2 and 5 digit lists, building societies are the largest job chunk (1200 / 0.85%) and have been concentrated in Bradford (near the top of UK concentration) for the full data time period. There are actually more jobs in the ‘banks’ category (2400, 1,8%) but that is only a little above the GB average. Financial auxiliary jobs have been shrinking in number, and statistically more than most other places (Figure 15) and insurance is a very small number of jobs.
    • Companies House data also shows strong jobs growth in the last year (only Wakefield ahead in WY) - 14 firms with 10+ employees going from 1434 to 1558 employees (an 8.65% increase, Figure 9). (CH data also shows over 300 “fewer than 10 employees”) firms / lone traders, Figure 10).
  • Legal and accounting is a stand-out related sector in the PBS (Professional and Business Services) category (average 3.2% of jobs in 2021-23, 1.8% of total). With historically very high output per FT worker in Bradford (Figure 2 panel 5), it has dropped more recently but is still very high - 93.5K per FT versus 78.4K average for other ITL3 zones.
    • The sector has grown jobs only modestly over the data time period, but it started from a high GVA and jobs baseline. Digging into the 5 digit SICs for the sector, ‘accounting/auditing’ (1150 jobs in latest BRES year) has been growing its job concentration strongly - still below average GB concentration, but heading higher and very high output. BRES records 833 workers in ‘solicitors’, but the trajectory is not as strong as accounting.
    • Companies House data for the last year shows growth in “fewer than 10 employee” firms of 7.4% (now up to 670 employees), though 10+ employee firms have been fairly static.

Notes on other service sectors

  • Real estate has a high output per worker everywhere (with imputed rent removed, so this is purely real estate itself, not a reflection of rising house prices). This should arguably be in “strongest service sectors”, but it is difficult to discern from the data where the productivity is coming from. Exactly half of the job count in this sector is in ‘housing association renting’. Jobs are increasing in each of the other subsectors (real estate, agencies, other letting) which are another 1000 jobs and, looking in Companies House data, there are firms like Dacre, Son and Hartley who employ 100 people and are clearly strong. Job numbers have risen since 2015/17 but output hasn’t risen quite as quickly (see GVA vs jobs plot, Figure 31). The sector is also productive even by UK standards (195K per FT worker versus 167K). But in this data, it cannot be seen what role housing associations versus other real estate is playing in this.
  • It is tricky to define architecture/engineering/tech testing’s place in Bradford (SIC 71). It is a large chunk of Bradford’s economy - more than 2000 jobs, 1.5% of full time workforce in latest BRES year, and has historically kept around the same number. However - while its GVA per worker in Figure 2 was very high in 2015-17, it has dropped off constantly since (while other places in WY have slowly climbed), now approaching the same output per worker as head offices / consultancy (though it is still above the average of other ITL3s). Jobs in the sector have grown in most places, but Bradford is some way into the group where GVA has dropped (Figure 23). There has been a jump in job numbers in the most recent data but no GVA jump, suggesting productivity continues to decline. Companies House shows a large number of SMEs in the sector, with modest jobs growth. But this sector (despite technically being under a ‘services’ heading) is obviously important to manufacturing and other productive output.
    • What factors underpin this steady slope? This data isn’t able to say directly, but identifying what’s happening here could be a first step to addressing it.
  • Utilities and power (5.7% of Bradford’s GVA, 2500 jobs in BRES) are tricky sectors to analyse. While the sector appears highly productive in Bradford (187K per FT job versus 138K GB average), differences in this sector largely come down to the capital intensity of the sector. There can be innovative frontier firms in these SIC codes, but the fact that very few firms in these sectors appear in the Companies House data suggests this sector is largely external utilities in the area, and could be difficult to affect.
  • Research / advertising, while apparently a high productivity sector, is fewer than 300 jobs, and this SIC misses other places where important research activity takes place.
  • Other professional, scientific and technical activities (“Other PSTech”, so any other apart from head offices, consultancy, legal, engineering - see the BRES job treemap for ‘professional/sci/techn’) appears productive, but is only a very small number of jobs. Bradford has been following the same trajectory as West Yorkshire, output per worker growing, and this is bucking the national trend where in places with more than 1% GVA in this sector, jobs have grown but productivity dropped. But it’s a very small sector.
  • Office administration and business support activities (“Admin” in the plots below; distinct from “public admin”) is not highly productive but its trajectory is worth noting. As Figure 32 shows, its jobs/GVA combination has led to a low but steady GVA per FT job increase (where a lot of other places have seen declines). The fact that this sector is so many jobs (3200 average 2021-23), and its connections with other PBS sectors, makes it worth noting.

Manufacturing / production sectors

Despite an overall decline in the proportion of Bradford’s broad manufacturing economy (in line with the most of the rest of the UK and West Yorkshire, Figure 1), there is important production sector activity to highlight.

Strongest sectors

  • The machinery and transport manufacture sector (SIC 28-30, ~2.5% of jobs in 2021-23 average) has been growing in productivity since 2015/17, on a quite different upward trajectory from the rest of West Yorkshire (Figure 2 panel 4). It has been growing as a proportion of GVA and jobs in Bradford and its GVA has been rising steadily in the ‘increasing productivity’ zone (Figure 25) where most other places have seen productivity drops. It is now at or near the UK average sector productivity - a rare position for a Northern manufacturing sector (though Companies House data for the last year doesn’t show quite as strong performance).
    • Two thirds of these jobs are in machinery manufacturing, and every 5 digit subsector in that is increasing in concentration (only a tiny proportion in motor vehicles, according to BRES).
  • Metals sectors (SIC 2 digit 13-15) has a high LQ and ~2.6% of Bradford jobs (Figure 3). It has had continuous GVA and jobs growth - an average of 4.1% yearly GVA growth (1.1-6.9%, Figure 14) from 2015, increasing more since 2019. This covers many cutting-edge firms like Produmax, who classify themselves as ‘manufacture of other fabricated metal products’. (The 5 digit SIC jobs breakdown for fabricated metals shows this category is where the largest chunk of the fab metals jobs are in Bradford; BRES doesn’t record many jobs in basic metals).
    • However, the metals sector has low average output per worker. As the 2D GVA/jobs plots for metals shows (Figure 24), the sector has maintained an output increase alongside its productivity - right in the centre of other places seeing productivity losses and gains. This mean its productivity (gva/job) hasn’t actually changed much, while job and GVA values have both increased by more than 60% since 2015/17.
  • Textiles continue to grow GVA and jobs concentration (GVA more than jobs, Figure 3) though the sector isn’t quite 1% of total full time jobs. Companies House data, however, suggests the sector is probably larger than BRES’ survey is picking up on - there are nearly 1000 jobs in apparel firms in CH (BRES finds only 20) - so it may well be more like 2% of total jobs.
    • Historically, it has been higher than average GVA per job for Bradford, with all West Yorkshire authorities very close in output - but this has declined since COVID (Figure 2) and is now almost exactly average for Bradford but a little below UK average for the sector. The 2D GVA/job plot for textiles (Figure 26) shines some light on this. In the very few UK places where textiles are more than 1% of the economy, none have better productivity in 2021/23 than they did in 2015/17. Some (e.g. Calderdale/Kirklees) have seen jobs increase while GVA has dropped. Along with Blackburn, Bradford is doing relatively well on this metric - growth continues though with apparent productivity issues.

Notes on other manufacturing/production sectors

  • As Bradford’s largest 2-digit sector by job count, Food/beverage manufacturing has 3.6% of full time employers according to BRES, and 2.5% of its GVA. Worker growth looks especially strong in the last year in the Companies House data: the 14 firms with 10+ employees have grown nearly 9% (Figure 9) and 74 microfirms with fewer than 10 employees have gone from 212 to 281 employees (Figure 10), both higher than the rest of West Yorkshire. However, the sector overall has seen declining productivity, and Bradford’s GVA per worker is at the lower end for West Yorkshire (along with Leeds - the other authorities are higher; Figure 2). As the GVA vs jobs 2D plot for the sector shows (Figure 34), most places have seen job increases with either GVA shrinkage or too slow GVA growth for productivity gains; Bradford’s GVA growth in the sector has been static.
  • Bradford’s chemicals industry (SIC 19-23) has a similar pattern to architecture/engineering/tech testing - except in chemicals, job numbers continue to grow while GVA drops. Jobs have yo-yo’d but have been mostly rising in recent years - but GVA has dropped, leading to worsening productivity. Demand issues in recent years accounts for some of this (see e.g. this report) - but this pattern of GVA decline has been in progress from 2015-17. 5 digit chemical sectors are mostly seeing jobs growth - rubber/plastic jobs increasing strongly (700+ now in two subsectors) and chemical manufacturing jobs in most subsectors continue to grow too. But - see Figure 33 - Bradford has seen one of the largest output drops in GB (while, as everywhere else, seeing job numbers rise). This is clearly a national issue, but Bradford seems especially vulnerable.
  • Construction of buildings (“Buildings” in the plots, SIC 41) is worth noting for its improving productivity (GVA growing faster than jobs but both growing, Figure 29). The West Yorkshire pattern in Figure 2 (panel 3) for output per full time job shows the region moving together, though Bradford is at the bottom of the sector’s GVA per worker here - but it is also following the recent rise seen across the region.

ONS GVA AND JOBS DATA

Structural / broad sector GVA change

GVA per full time job over time: Bradford v other ITL3 zones for 2 digit SIC sectors

These plots use linked regional GVA and BRES jobs data to examine productivity, output and job changes. To make this link, sector categories have to be reduced to those in the ONS GVA data (46 categories rather than the full 88 two digit SICs).

These are measured in GVA per full time job (GVA / jobs), using inflation-adjusted (chained volume) GVA.

BRES does contain more detail - more on that below and in the separate BRES 5 digit breakdown.

GVA per job as a measure needs some caution: output is also due to other productive factors, so high capital intensity sectors (power, telecoms, other ICT though not all) can appear overly productive per worker. It’s more representative for service sectors where an employee’s output more clearly correlates to total output, such as legal services.

Location quotients for GVA and jobs in 2 digit SIC sectors: most recent and change over time

These LQ plots show location quotients for linked GVA and jobs data for Bradford, overlaid on top of LQs for all other ITL3 zones, to give a sense of what the spread is across Great Britain.

A location quotient is the ratio of a place’s sector proportion to its national proportion. So e.g. if manufacturing in Bradford made up 20% of its economy compared to maufacturing being 10% of the UK economy, its LQ in Bradford would be 2.

Here’s an explanation of how to read these location quotient plots:

  • Location quotients for sectoral GVA in the selected region
  • Sectors to the right of the blue line are more concentrated in this region when compared to the national average. (e.g. LQ = 10, it is 10x more concentrated). To the left are less concentrated than nationally.
  • Green circles have been increasing in concentration over time (larger circles increasing the most)
  • Red circles have been decreasing in concentration over time (larger circles decreasing the most)
  • Black horizontal lines show the min and max of concentration in the data’s history (from 2015 to 2023) (so, often, large green circles will be at the right of this line because sector concentration has been steadily increasing)
  • Per sector GVA money amounts and percent of that region’s entire economy are shown on the right.

GROWTH TRENDS IN GVA, JOBS AND GVA PER JOB

This section examines growth trends in Bradford for GVA and GVA per full time job (a productivity measure) (both using the 46 sector categories available in the GVA data), as well as growth in jobs using BRES data (a larger list of 88 SIC 2 digit sectors).

NOTE: There’s a section right at the end that digs more deeply into some selected sectors from this section (“Closer look at jobs vs GVA percent change in selected sectors”), taken from the GVA vs job percent change 2D plots catalogue page.

First, this is done for the full range of data - 2015 to 2023. Second, it’s repeated for the more recent time period from 2019 to 2023, to look for trends changes across the pandemic and more recently.

Here’s an explanation of what’s in the plots below.

  • Linear slopes are found for each sector in Bradford, and each is compared to (a) all other sectors in Bradford (“Bradford internal”, light blue) and (b) the same sector in all other ITL3s zones in the data (“Bradford to other places”, dark blue).
  • For example - looking at the most obvious stand-out sector in the first plot is head offices / consultancy. Its GVA has grown significantly more than around 90% of other sectors in Bradford, and significantly more than around 70% of the head office / consultancy sector in other ITL3 zones. Its productivity (measured in GVA per full time job) has grown more than around 43% of other ITL3 zones.
  • The x axis gives the percent of slopes that are significantly different to others (i.e. 95% confidence intervals don’t overlap).
  • The vertical axis lists all sectors in alphabetical order. These labels contain the following information:
    • Green text: slope rises over time - growth is on average going up
    • Red: slope drops over time - growth is on average going down
    • Bold green and red: the up or down slope is significantly different from zero (faded red and green can’t be statitically separated from no change over time).
    • Text after the sector name is percent change per year on average with 95% confidence intervals.

So:

  • To see whether a sector has been growing or shrinking overall, use the sector text (green for growth, red for shrinkage, values given for average % change per year)
  • To compare that sector’s trend with other sectors in Bradford or the same sector in other places, use the bars in the plot. Note - these are relative: a slope could be relatively more positive than another, but the sector overall could still be shrinking. For example, ICT has been growing strongly in most places in the UK - but is growth in one place significantly stronger than others? That’s what the place and sector slope comparisons try to answer.

UK INDUSTRIAL STRATEGY LINKAGE

The UK Industrial Strategy includes a sector definition list that lists sectors (of differing specificity / SIC code number) that the government considers part of its target sectors. As that page says, most of these SIC codes cannot capture the full range of possible firms the strategy aims to cover, and it also contains entire sections that SIC codes can’t cover at all - clean energy and certain digital sectors in particular.

However, it does allow an analysis of which sectors are definitely within those industrial strategy categories. This section gathers job details for those specific sectors - a distinct range of types and breadth - and compares Bradford to the rest of the UK where possible, to see which sectors align with the industrial strategy and other analysis above. This is done for both BRES and Companies House job counts, to pick up on different job types.

The industrial strategy drills down below its top level sectors into ‘frontier’ sectors, usually (but not always) using more granular SIC codes. The plots below break the data down into these frontier and other sectors using location quotients.

LQs are useful here as they show whether a sector has a stronger presence in a place regardlesss of the sector size or SIC digit level.

Only industrial strategy sectors with 100+ jobs in Bradford are shown (average from 2021 to 2023 for BRES, and in the latest accounts for Companies House).

Note also: job percents for Bradford show percent of just total industrial strategy jobs, not percent of all jobs.
